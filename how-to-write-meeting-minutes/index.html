How to write meeting minutes

By Matt Kerr, who makes Mind the Minutes in Grand Rapids · Checked 9 November 2026

Minutes record what a group decided, not everything it said. Write down the date, time and place, who attended, each motion with who made and seconded it, how each vote went, and when the meeting ended. Then check the draft against your notes or the recording, and give it to the group to approve.

That works for any meeting. Boards have more to get right, because their minutes are the official record. This guide is for small boards: townships, villages, library and school boards, HOAs, churches and nonprofits. Where Michigan's Open Meetings Act sets a rule for public bodies, the section is cited.

Before the meeting

Build the draft from the agenda. Each agenda item becomes a heading. Fill in the board name, date, place and the list of members now, so in the meeting you only tick names and write motions.

Decide how you will capture the meeting. Notes alone are fine for a short agenda. A recording lets you check a vote or a mover afterwards.

During the meeting

Write the opening and the roll call

Note the time the meeting was called to order and who called it. List the members present and the members absent, since MCL 15.269(1) asks for both. If a member walks in late or leaves early, write the time. A trustee who arrives at 7:04 p.m. can vote at 7:05 p.m., and the minutes should show when they came in.

Write every motion the same way

Each motion needs five things: what was moved, who moved it, who seconded it, how the board voted and the result. Write the motion as it was stated. If you did not catch the wording, ask the chair to repeat it before the vote. That is easier than guessing a week later.

The Act defines a decision as a vote or other action on a motion, resolution, ordinance or similar measure (MCL 15.262(d)). Any decision made in the open meeting goes in the minutes.

Record roll-call votes by name

For a voice vote, the result is enough. For a roll call, write each member's name and vote. The Act says the minutes must include all roll-call votes (MCL 15.269(1)). Write an abstention as an abstention, not as a no, and add the count at the end, such as "carried 3 to 1, with 1 abstaining".

Summarize talk, don't transcribe it

One sentence on the main point of a discussion is usually plenty. Leave out side talk and opinions about people. Public comment is not on the Act's list of what minutes must show. One plain way to record it is the number of speakers and their topics, without quotes.

Closed sessions: record the vote, not the talk

In Michigan, calling a closed session takes a two-thirds roll-call vote of the members elected or appointed and serving, except for a few purposes the Act names (MCL 15.267(1)). Count it yourself. On a 5-member board, two-thirds of 5 rounds up to 4 yes votes, even if only 4 members are in the room. The allowed purposes, such as pending litigation, are listed in MCL 15.268.

The open minutes show the roll-call vote and the purpose. They say nothing about what was discussed. The clerk or a designated secretary keeps a separate set of closed-session minutes, which are not public. Those may be destroyed 1 year and 1 day after approval of the minutes of the regular meeting at which the closed session was approved (MCL 15.267(2)).

After the meeting: write it up and flag the gaps

Type the draft the same day or the next, while you still remember who said what. Put "Proposed minutes, not yet approved" at the top.

The most useful habit is marking what you are not sure of, instead of filling it in. A gap that you flag gets fixed at the next meeting. A guess gets approved and becomes the record.

Here is a worked example. It is a made-up 2-minute test meeting in which the second was mumbled and nobody said a clock time aloud. This line is from its transcript:

[00:01:10] Unknown Speaker: Someone said second, I think.

On 9 November 2026, Mind the Minutes's transcript tool turned that meeting into a draft. It did not invent a seconder. It wrote this:

Motion 1
Motion to approve the agenda.
Moved by: Casey Brown.
Seconded by: [CHECK] Unknown.
Voice vote: passed.

[CHECK] Call-to-order clock time was not spoken. Confirm the start time in meeting details.
[CHECK] Motion 1: a possible second needs review.
[CHECK] Adjournment clock time was not spoken. Review its transcript timestamp.
[CHECK] Motion 1: seconder is unclear or missing.

That is 4 items to check from a meeting of 7 lines. Doing it by hand works the same way. Put [CHECK] or a highlight on anything you would not swear to, then clear each one against your notes, the recording or the chair before you send the draft out.

Get them approved and posted on time

The board approves the minutes at a later meeting. Corrections are made at the next meeting after the one the minutes cover. The corrected minutes must show both the original entry and the correction, and they must be available at or before the next meeting after the correction (MCL 15.269(1)).

Michigan public bodies must make proposed minutes available within 8 business days after the meeting, and approved minutes within 5 business days after the meeting that approves them (MCL 15.269(3)). The Act's definitions in MCL 15.262 do not define "business day".

Thanksgiving week shows why the count matters. For a meeting on Monday, 23 November 2026, the safe date for proposed minutes is Thursday, 3 December 2026. That counts every Monday to Friday and skips no holidays. If your office was closed on both Thanksgiving Day and the day after, you may have until Monday, 7 December. If the board approves the minutes on Monday, 14 December 2026, the approved minutes' safe date is Monday, 21 December 2026.

One more rule matters most to school boards. Minutes must not include personally identifiable information that, if released, would keep the board from complying with FERPA, the federal student records privacy law (MCL 15.269(4)).
